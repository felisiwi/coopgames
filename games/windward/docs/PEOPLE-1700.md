# People and details of 1700 (research for the story)

Research for the 1700 era (the end of "New border", Great Northern War from 1700): the pilots and Torhild's world, a poor fishing hamlet, what the
war did to ordinary people and Tordenskiold's later raids, and storms. Machine-readable twin: `data/people-1700.json` (sources + items, flagged).
Builds on `docs/WORLD-1700.md` (cited as "WORLD-1700") and `story/characters/torhild-1700.md`. Read 2026-11-06. New sources: A01-A18 (pilots),
B01-B21 (hamlet), C01-C24 (war), D01-D10 (storms).

**How to read the flags.** `documented` = a source I read says it. `derived` = our arithmetic or a plain reading of documented facts. `inferred` =
reasoned from documented facts. `later-recorded` = a belief or practice written down 1740-1990; it is NOT proof for 1700. `INVENTED` = a suggestion
for the game, never history; each says which real source it is grounded in. "search-summary" = only a search snippet was seen: weak. Real named
people appear only where a source names them; no pilot, father, grandmother or boat is named by us.
The four drafts were written by research agents. **I re-fetched and re-read these myself against their sources:** the Lysekil pilot station (lotsarnastockholm.se:
"inrättad tidigt 1700 tal", 4 pilots in 1729), Käringön (Olof Eriksson Ström piloting Norwegian ships around 1710 with Peder Wessel aboard; the Lysekil
supervisor Peter Nilsson, appointed 1700, quitting in 1714), the "1736" lee-shore sentence, Olof Strömstierna's birth on Käringön 1664 (sv.wikipedia), and the
captured-sailor passage at Dynekilen (sv.wikipedia). The rest is as the agents report it (first-hand reading of the cited pages; weaker ones are marked).

## What this changes in the design (read this first)

1. **Torhild's family has no documented station.** The only pilot station in the game's box with a source is **Lysekil** ("early 1700s"); **Käringön** and
   **Gullholmen** (just outside or at the edge) had pilots from the 1690s-1700s. **Grundsund, Skaftö, Fiskebäckskil and Härmanö have no source for a pilot
   station**, and a 1940 list of pilot places leaves Grundsund out. The 1696 regulation mostly concerned Stockholm-Kalmar; on the west coast only the
   larger harbours were regulated. So Torhild's father piloting out of Grundsund is **plausible but informal**: a fisherman who knows the passage, not a
   station with a roll. Options: keep Grundsund and call it informal (recommended; true to the thin evidence), or move her family to Lysekil or Käringön
   (documented stations; Käringön has real named pilots, so you would have to avoid those names).
2. **The most useful true fact for Torhild: pilots were paid by the ship.** A quiet season meant no income, so the family fished and farmed a small croft too.
   A war year was the worst of both: fewer foreign ships, and Norwegians pressing pilots to work for them (section 1 and 3).
3. **There is almost nothing documented on pilots' wives, daughters or grandmothers.** What Torhild knows and does is INVENTED, grounded in how a pilot's
   family worked (pilotage was often inherited, apprentices were taken, wives and children ran the croft when the man was at sea).
4. **A true "phantom pilot" hook exists:** at Dynekilen (8 July 1716) Tordenskiold's force got hold of a Swedish sailor who had earlier piloted the Swedish
   transport fleet into the narrow fjord. His name is not given anywhere. This is the cleanest real bridge from Torhild's world to the Tordenskiold era.
5. **Storms: nothing documented hit Bohuslän in 1650-1725.** The Great Storm of 1703 and the Christmas Flood of 1717 are North Sea disasters; no source I read
   puts either on this coast. The honest approach is an INVENTED local gale (section 4).
6. **Beliefs are mostly 20th-century.** Gast, fej, taboos and the havsrå are recorded from informants born 1859-1915. The only c.1742 material is Kalm's
   weather signs, which fishermen and pilots told him. Use the later material as flavour, flagged.
7. **Correction to the design/brief notes:** Lars Gathenhielm, the famous privateer, worked from **Gothenburg**, not Marstrand (sv.wikipedia, SBL).

## 1. Pilots around 1700 (Torhild's world)

### Facts (sourced)

- **The law.** Lotshemman (pilot farms) go back to Gustav Vasa: holders got tax relief for piloting crown ships and recruiting new pilots; pilotage often
  passed down in families (A01, SOU 2007:106). 1667 Sjölag: compulsory pilotage where a guide was needed, aimed mainly at foreign ships (A01). The 1696
  regulation (19 Sept) put pilotage under the Admiralty, required Swedish subjects, an **exam and an oath for pilots and for lotsdrängar (journeymen)**, with
  penalties from fines up to nine *gatlopp* (running the gauntlet) or death (A01). Every pilot had to take an apprentice (A03).
- **For our region the system was thin.** SOU says the late-1600s ordinances mainly concerned Stockholm-Kalmar; the south and west coast were regulated "only
  at the larger harbours" (A01). Western and Northern districts were created only in 1724 (A02).
- **Pay and status.** Pay was per ship (*lotspenning*), by draught, distance and fairway; some state payments were "more alms than pay" (A04, A03). Holders of a
  lotshemman farmed and fished and were exempt from ground tax (A03, A04, not dated to 1700). Pilots first used their own fishing or rowing boats (A03).
- **Lysekil.** Pilot station "set up early in the 1700s"; **4 pilots and 2 apprentices in 1729**, 4 pilots and 4 lotsdrängar in 1744 (A07, re-read by me). A
  Lysekil supervisor, **Peter Nilsson** (appointed 1700), had to stop in **1714**: he said Norwegians ruined him in 1711 when he refused to cooperate (A08, re-read).
- **Käringön.** Station first on a roll in 1717, but **Olof Eriksson Ström** (born about 1640) was a pilot in the 1690s and **around 1710 piloted Norwegian ships
  with Peder Wessel (later Tordenskiold) aboard**; the source guesses his sympathies stayed Norwegian from his youth (Käringön was Norwegian until 1658), and
  that money played a role (A08, re-read). 1729: 3 pilots, 1 lotsdräng and a lotsålderman (A08).
- **Gullholmen.** Pilots stationed from "early 1700s"; the earliest named pilot there is in service 1721 (A09). A 1610 tax roll lists 24 strandsittare, who
  the source infers owed pilotage for their tax exemption (A09, B01).
- **Marstrand.** 1697 regulation: 6 pilots and 2 lotsdrängar for the harbour (A10).
- **Nearest rich record (analogue only): Brännö, Gothenburg.** A "stuva" (cottage) on a rock or on a bit of land a farmer could spare; 1665 two Brännö men were
  fined and whipped for piloting an English ship from a plague port; pilots were expected to keep sea marks; boats were lost (1782, 1801) (A06). Outside the region.
- **Sea marks.** Karl IX in 1606 ordered marks and beacons ("vrethar") on Vinga and Galtö, Gothenburg (A06). A *kummel* is a white-painted stone mound and a
  *bak* a wooden triangle, "among the oldest sea marks in Scandinavia" (A05, undated). The nearest light was **Nidingen** in Halland, a coal fire from the
  1620s-40s, south of the region (A04, A05). No documented marks, leading lines or pilot signals for the game's coast around 1700 (WORLD-1700 says the same).
- **A period phrase for dialogue.** A 1736 definition quoted on the Käringön page: "Et Skepp är på lägervall, när vinden ligger mitt åt landet, och det blåser så
  hårdt, at man intet kan lofvera ifrån landet" (a ship is on a lee shore when the wind blows straight onto land, so hard that it cannot tack away). It is a
  period seamanship definition, not a pilot's saying (A08, re-read).

### Uncertain / conflicting

- The date of the ban on unauthorised pilotage: 1671 (A01, A03) against 1674 (A14).
- Käringön: the station roll starts 1717, but individuals are named for the 1690s and 1711; not a real conflict.
- The 1736 sentence is quoted inside a 19th-century wreck account on the page; its original source is not given.

### Not found

- Any pilot station, lookout or beacon-fire site at Grundsund, Skaftö, Fiskebäckskil or Härmanö; any beacon (*vårdkase*) sites in Bohuslän in 1700.
- Signals to call a pilot (flags, shots, fires) c.1700 on the west coast; named leading marks for Lysekil or Grundsund; pilot pay at Lysekil or Käringön c.1700.
- Anything on pilots' wives, daughters, grandmothers, or the design of a pilot's cottage in 1700.
- A Bohuslän dialect list with maritime words (the sv.wikipedia list is unsourced and has none; Ernby's thesis on Bohuslän fish names exists but was not opened).

### What Torhild would plausibly know and do (INVENTED, grounded in A01, A03, A04, A06, A08)

- Her father is a pilot (**name not found; not guessed**) and also fishes. In a quiet season money is short; she helps with the lines, the croft and the boat.
- She knows the fairway **by transit marks and rock names** learned on the water (a notch in a skerry, a house, the church), not from a chart. In the lab she can
  teach "line up the cairn and the notch". Grounded in the documented kummel/bak and in the absence of any chart; the specific marks are invented.
- Pilots had to be examined and sworn: a daughter would know the **fear of a mistake** (fines, the gauntlet). This gives a reason for her to bend her father's
  rules and still stay careful.
- She can **row and sail the family boat** and mend gear. A daughter in the pilot's job itself has no source: that is the game's choice, not history.
- Torhild's wish "to see beyond the flashing fog" fits a coast with **no lights at all**: the first light in the whole west was Nidingen far to the south.
- The grandmother (name open in Torhild's file) runs the croft and tells the old stories. Grounded in the Fjällbacka note that wives and children ran the farm
  when the men were at sea (A17, search-summary). Her stories can use the later-recorded lore in section 2, flagged.

## 2. A poor fishing hamlet around 1700

### Facts (sourced)

- **Who lived there.** Strandsittare: families who rented the ground under their house and paid a yearly *grundlega* ("six marks of silver coin" originally); the house
  counted as movable property and could be moved (B02, B17 = an 1899 report, so the legal wording is late). Gullholmen: 24 strandsittare (1610), about 39 households
  (1645), **20 fishing families at the end of the 1600s** (B01; the last figure is secondhand from an 1840s book).
- **Houses.** Fishers had little land, so houses stood "close, sometimes very close" on rock and shore plots that landowners valued little (B03). Excavations at
  Skållehus on Stora Härmanö show **permanent fisher habitation from the 1500s into the 1700s**, in a place that is not in the tax rolls (B02). A 7 x 7 m two-room
  house with a stone floor and a tile stove was found at Skioldenborg (late 1500s, a better-off house; **not** a typical poor hut) (B16).
- **Vanishing hamlets.** Many strandsittar places vanished, "often around the early 1700s": Härmanösund, Vallerö and Lyreleje, which the source explains by heavy
  tax, lost trade and wartime plundering (B02). Visual hook: the remains of simple shore buildings ("tomtningar"), a rounded stone bank, sometimes with hearth traces (B02).
- **War and tax.** In the Gyldenløve war (1675-79) houses were burned and boats stolen in coastal places; the crown forbade peasant sailing and for a time all trade
  had to go through Gothenburg (B02). Great Northern War: 1716-19 landings (*strandhugg*), captured cargoes and kidnapped locals; Tordenskiold anchored at Gullholmen
  several times (B02).
- **A good true story about a "legend".** Gullholmen's big harbour wreck was always called "Tordenskiold's ship", but tree-ring dating gives a latest ring of 1717
  and felling **after 1727**, so the legend is later (B01).
- **Boats.** The only dated Bohuslän fishing boat I found is the *tosejlare* ("two-sailer"), **built about 1700-1850**, for hook-and-line fishing and fish cargo, no
  shelter or heating, sailing as far as Skagen to buy plaice (B14). A *hogdalseka* (a small clinker rowing eka that "may have had a sprit sail") exists, but the
  museum boat is 20th-century (B15). So the game's tarred eka with a spritsail is plausible, not documented for 1700 (WORLD-1700 and BOAT-BRIEF already say this).
- **Timber was scarce.** Bohuslän's oak and pine had been heavily cut in the 1500s-1600s; Kalm in 1742 describes bald hills (B01, B02). Fuel and boat timber were
  dear (derived).
- **Language and church.** After 1658 people kept the old laws and church order; church language stayed Danish until after the Gyldenløve war, while everyday speech
  stayed the old south-Norwegian-like dialect (B02). Käringön and Gullholmen got churches only in the 1700s herring period, so people went to a parish church on a
  larger island (inferred).
- **Weather lore from fishermen and pilots, recorded 1742.** Pehr Kalm, travelling in Bohuslän in 1742, wrote down signs from "fishermen and pilots": mist coiling round
  the hills means storm from S, SW or W (Morlanda); a roaring sea after calm shows where the wind will come from; two cloud banks in the SW and NE "weigh against each other"
  until one wins; a clear sky two or three days ahead shows the wind's quarter (B06, OCR unproofread). This is the best sourced weather and fog lore in the file.

### What people feared and believed (all LATER-RECORDED unless marked)

- **From the 1900s, collected from a man born in Grundsund/Skaftö in 1915 (B04, B05):** a drowned man not buried in consecrated ground could appear as a *gast*; a
  painting shows a half-naked gast looking at dusk for his clothes in a heap of wreck goods; women climbed the rocks to watch for boats; a boat coming in with the top of
  the mainsail lowered meant someone had died; a rooster carried to crow over a drowned body; touching the anchor chain and saying "Gud styrke dej i natt käre vän".
  Departure taboos (the wife must not do much housework or she might "sweep out" her husband); big knives (*fläckknivar*) slashed through the air to drive spirits off;
  *fej* (an omen of death, such as seeing a crew wading in a stream); *fejmansväder* = weather bad enough to lose vessels; gastar crying from the sea before a change of
  weather; drowned sailors laid in stone cairns that the gastar then haunted. The earliest dated source in that article is a priest in 1746.
- **Havsrå / sea woman:** a Bohuslän version collected from a man born in Gothenburg in 1859 says she appears at night or before storms, glows, and lures men to drown
  on the skerries; protection is rowan or steel, and you say nothing until morning (B07, a secondary website that cites sources I did not check). Swedish Wikipedia
  treats *sjörå* as a lake spirit (B08). **Do not call her "Sjöfruen" at sea without flagging this.**
- **Not Bohuslän:** the *draug* is Norwegian (first recorded 1729) (B11); *irrbloss* and *lyktgubbe* are bog and graveyard lights (B09, B10). A phantom light at sea
  would be INVENTED. The nearest real basis is the gast that cries out before weather (B05) plus the lantern-man who leads a lost traveller home for a coin (B10).
- **Real fears c.1700 (documented):** war raids and plundering (B02), tax and ruin (B02), the loss of trade, storms and winter ice (B14). The rest is later-recorded.

### Uncertain / conflicting

- Gullholmen's age: tourist pages say roots in the 1200s; the museum says the earliest sure evidence is 1588 and doubts the 1260s identification (B01).
- Herring periods: 1660-1680 (B13, B18) against a mid-1600s period "not nearly as extensive" as the 1500s one (B02); the big period starts 1747/1748/1752 depending on source. The
  project already treats c.1700 as lean; B02 supports that for 1680-1747 but says nothing explicit about 1690-1740.
- When the strandsittar places vanished: "often around the early 1700s" (B02) against a Brattö article (search-summary only) reading the change as shrinking shore activity (B21).
- "6 daler" at Grebbestad in 1697 (B17): silver or copper currency unclear.

### Not found

- **Houses c.1700** (size, roof, one room, hearth, animals under the same roof): only B16 (1500s, better-off), B03, B02. Dalén 1941 is a scanned PDF that needs OCR.
- **Food** (barley, bread, milk, salt fish; whether potatoes had reached Bohuslän) and **clothing**: nothing sourced.
- **Fishing gear c.1700** (long line, nets, fish traps, lobster): only Kalm's hooks and bait (B06), the tosejlare's hook-and-line (B14) and a beach-seine eka (B15). The 1752 and 1774 fishery inquiries were blocked.
- **Boat names** (färja, skötbåt, lodbåt, jakt) c.1650-1750; women's and children's work; school; the priest's role; tithe.
- **Skeppsgast, a sea "Sjöfru", fire on the sea, wreck-law attitudes (*strandrätt*), taboo words at sea:** no readable source.

## 3. The Great Northern War on this coast; Tordenskiold as a hook

### Facts (sourced)

- **What reached ordinary people (documented in B02, C20):** extra taxes and forced provisions, landings and plunder in 1716-19, captured cargoes, kidnapped locals,
  Swedish transports to Strömstad as prize targets, a Swedish skans at Svanesund 1719, and a long peace only after 1720. A conscription of båtsmän from 1704 (C19, search-summary):
  Strömstierna led the first Bohuslän båtsman company from 1704 (C11).
- **Pilots under pressure.** Around 1710-14 Lysekil and Käringön show Norwegians pressing pilots (section 1, A08). Treat as a hook, not a general rule: the page says
  "similar cases were probably not rare" but gives only these.
- **Dynekilen, 8 July 1716 (new style; 28 June old style).** Tordenskiold's Dano-Norwegian force entered a fjord about 100 m wide in places and destroyed Olof
  Strömstierna's Swedish transport fleet carrying siege artillery to Karl XII's army; Karl XII gave up on Fredriksten (C01, C02, C03). **They had caught a Swedish
  sailor who had piloted the Swedish transports in; no one on the Norwegian side had been there** (C01, C03). The pilot is unnamed.
  Koster fishermen reportedly told the Danes about the fleet and the officers' wedding at the Dyne farm; the source reads this as weak loyalty to Sweden (one secondary
  page citing Kavli 1990, **not seen**) (C03).
- **Ships (documented, counts differ by source).** Dano-Norwegian: *Hjælperen* (Tordenskiold's flagship in the fight), *Arca Noae*, *Hvide Ørn* (the ex-Swedish *Hvita Örn*,
  captured 1715), *Vindhunden*, galleys. Swedish: *Stenbocken* (struck her colours), the galley *Wrede* (Strömstierna's flag), and others (C01-C04). Tordenskiold was ennobled
  in 1716 (C04).
- **Olof Strömstierna (born Knape, 29 Jan 1664 - 17 Sep 1730).** sv.wikipedia (re-read by me): born on **Käringön**, son of the Marstrand burgher Nils Knape; guarded the Bohuslän
  skerries 1709-13; vice-admiral after Dynekilen; organised supplies to Strömstad in 1718 (C11). en.wikipedia disagrees on birthplace and father (C12). The Käringön link is a local-pride hook.
- **Strömstad 1717:** Tordenskiold's attack failed (8 July old style = 19 July new style 1717); in 1718 Strömstad was Karl XII's base, with barracks, supply magazines and a galley road
  overland to the Idefjord (C06, C07, C17). Karl XII died 11 Dec 1718 (C07).
- **Marstrand and Carlsten, July 1719.** Landing on 10 July (old style), the Swedes scuttled five frigates (*Halmstad, Stettin, Kalmar, Fredricus, Charlotta*) on 11-12 July,
  officers signed the surrender 15 July; Colonel Danckwardt was executed 16 Sept 1719; Sweden got the fortress back in 1720 (C08, C09). Legends with no contemporary source:
  Tordenskiold scouting Marstrand disguised as a fisher; a small force made to look like a thousand (C08, C10).
- **Privateers.** Lars Gathenhielm (born 30 Nov 1689 Onsala, died 25 Apr 1718) operated from **Gothenburg** (letter of marque 14 June 1710; ennobled 1715) (C13, C14). Swedish
  privateer types from about 1709-10 included brigantines, huckers, a galliot and a frigate (C16).
- **A true "toast" scene (1714).** The Swedish privateer *Olbing Galley* fought Tordenskiold's *Løvendals Galej* off Lindesnes on 26-27 June 1714, no decision, and the captains
  exchanged toasts (C15). Norwegian coast, but shows the tone.
- **The flag.** The Danish naval ensign was swallow-tailed (the *splitflag*), regulated 1625 (C21, search-summary; fetches failed). The echo with the player's boat Svalan (the swallow) is true;
  the exact flags on any 1716-19 ship are **not verified**.

### Uncertain / conflicting

- Strömstierna's birthplace and father: sv.wikipedia (Käringön, Nils Knape) against en.wikipedia.
- Ship counts at Dynekilen differ by source; dates differ between old and new style throughout (use the old-style dates for Swedish sources, new style for Danish).
- The plague of 1710 is documented only for Skåne, Stockholm and Karlshamn, **not Bohuslän** (C24).
- The red shade and approval dates of the swallow-tail flag conflict.

### Not found

- Any named local pilot at Dynekilen; any source for *forced* pilotage by Tordenskiold.
- Any Tordenskiold raid or visit tied to Lysekil, Fiskebäckskil, Skaftö or Gullholmen (only a general Gullholmen anchorage in B02 and the "mest brugelige Havne" quote already in WORLD-1700).
- Billeting, requisition, beacon (*vårdkase*) watches and coastal refugees specific to our coast.
- The Bohuslän museum's war-time letters, Strömbom 1902 and Kavli 1990 (cited by others, not read).

### Hooks for a later era (1716-19), all grounded above

1. **The captured pilot** (C01, C03): Torhild's father or uncle has guided a convoy and fears being the next one taken. The pilot at Dynekilen is unnamed, so this character is INVENTED.
2. **Käringön's admiral** (C11): Strömstierna, a Käringön-born commander; Torhild may know his family. Name check: it is sourced, birthplace is disputed.
3. **The swallow-tail on the horizon** (C21): a splitflag ship at sea, and the ship names *Hjælperen*, *Hvide Ørn* (a captured Swedish ship that changed flags), *Vindhunden*.
4. **Scuttled frigates at Marstrand** (C08, C09): masts in the harbour, five named hulls; salvage by locals is INVENTED, the sinking is documented.
5. **The Strömstad road** (C07, C17): barracks, magazines, galleys on rollers; Karl XII's death in Dec 1718 as a rumour moving along the coast.
6. **A privateer-captain toast** (C15): a courteous enemy at sea, grounded in the Olbing Galley.
7. **Tordenskiold as a stranger in a fisher's boat** (legend, C08, C10): flag as legend if used.

## 4. Storms around 1700 (for the jump)

### Facts (sourced)

- **The Great Storm of 26-27 Nov 1703 (old style; 7 Dec new style)** hit England, the Channel, the Low Countries and northern Germany; KNMI says Scandinavia was under high pressure then (D01, D02).
  **Verdict: North Sea only.** One Wikipedia sentence says HMS *Association* was "blown from Harwich to Gothenburg"; a second page supports a ship driven out to sea; neither says anything about damage
  in Bohuslän (D01, D03 search-summary). It is a hook (a storm that drives a ship across to this coast), not evidence the storm struck here.
- **The Christmas Flood, 24-25 Dec 1717:** the damage was on the Dutch, German and Danish/Schleswig North Sea coasts; the article lists "Scandinavia" without detail, and nothing supports Bohuslän (D04, D05).
- **Cold years (documented for Europe, with no Bohuslän detail):** the famine of 1695-97, worst in Finland and Norrland (D08, search-summary), and the Great Frost of 1709 (D07).
- **Period texture from the 1703 storm:** Defoe, "No pen could describe it" and windmill brakes catching fire; Dutch accounts with lightning and tornado-like cells (D01, D02).

### Not found

- Any named great storm on the Bohuslän coast in 1650-1725; any shipwreck list for that coast and period (wreck databases, Bohusläns museum wreck lists); church-record storm notes; Bohuslän-specific winter or ice
  conditions c.1700; Swedish or Danish storm series for the period (they are known to be incomplete before about 1725, D06 search-summary).
- Local weather lore for c.1700 beyond Kalm's 1742 signs (section 2). Leads to check: ISOF and DigitaltMuseum.

### Recommendation (INVENTED, grounded in D01-D04)

Make the jump storm a **local gale, not a named historical storm**. If a date is wanted, echo late November 1703 (old style) as a mood without claiming it hit Bohuslän. The weather signs before it should be
Kalm's 1742 fisherman-and-pilot signs (mist coiling round the hills, a roaring sea after calm), flagged as 1742 and not 1700.
